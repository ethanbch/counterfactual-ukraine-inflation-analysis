The counterfactual, estimated via two independent methods : Ciccarelli-Mojon (2010) factor loading and Blanchard-Quah (1989) SVAR demand-shock replacement : consistently shows that Euro Area membership would have substantially lowered Ukrainian inflation during most crisis episodes. During the GFC (2008–09), the actual–counterfactual gap averaged +11.7pp (CM) and +9.8pp (SVAR), reflecting the inflationary cost of the UAH's 49% depreciation : an adjustment channel unavailable under euro membership. Both methods converge tightly, reinforcing the robustness of the estimate. The gap is largest during the Crimea/Donbas episode (2014–15), averaging +7.0pp under the CM method against a counterfactual of ~24.6%, as the cumulative 73% hryvnia collapse passed through entirely to consumer prices : a channel the ECB's nominal anchor would have mitigated. The SVAR produces a CF estimate of approximately −3% for this period, which is economically implausible and reflects a model limitation: the Chow-Lin-interpolated activity proxy fails to capture the structural break induced by the Donbas conflict, causing the demand-shock replacement to generate spurious negative values. The CM estimate is therefore retained as the primary reference for this episode. During the 2022 full-scale invasion, the gap narrows to −0.1pp (CM) and −2.3pp (SVAR), with the counterfactual slightly _above_ actual inflation. This counterintuitive result is economically meaningful: the Euro Area itself was simultaneously absorbing a large energy-price shock, so ECB membership would have provided little additional insulation. Moreover, the Ukrainian government's active price controls, fuel subsidies, and informal anchoring to Western aid compressed realised inflation below what an open-price EA-equivalent trajectory would have implied. Consistent with Barro and Gordon (1983) and Giavazzi and Pagano (1988), the credibility-import benefit is front-loaded: the pre-2016 average gap (+3.54pp) exceeds the post-2016 gap (+1.91pp), as the NBU's adoption of inflation targeting in August 2015 partially converged Ukraine's monetary framework toward the ECB norm, structurally reducing the treatment intensity of hypothetical euro membership. Finally, following De Grauwe (2012), it bears noting that Euro Area membership would not have been an unambiguous gain: in 2014 and 2022, Ukraine avoided deeper crises precisely because it could devalue and impose capital controls : instruments unavailable under the euro. Without a national lender of last resort, the asymmetric shocks of 2014 and 2022 might instead have triggered sovereign debt distress, shifting the inflationary cost into a fiscal-financial crisis of potentially greater severity.
