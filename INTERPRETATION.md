The counterfactual, estimated via two independent methods : Ciccarelli-Mojon (2010) factor loading and Blanchard-Quah (1989) SVAR demand-shock replacement : consistently shows that Euro Area membership would have substantially lowered Ukrainian inflation during most crisis episodes. During the GFC (2008–09), the actual-counterfactual gap averaged +11.7pp (CM) and +9.8pp (SVAR), reflecting the inflationary cost of the UAH's 49% depreciation, an adjustment channel unavailable under euro membership. Both methods converge tightly, reinforcing the robustness of the estimate. The Crimea/Donbas episode (2014–15) is more nuanced. The CM gap remains positive at +7.0pp, but the counterfactual level is relatively high because part of this episode coincides with the hard-peg regime, when the time-varying treatment is set to zero by construction. In those months, the counterfactual is intentionally pulled toward the actual series, reflecting the fact that Ukraine was already de facto subordinating monetary policy to an external anchor, so Euro Area membership would have added little extra discipline. The SVAR produces a counterfactual of roughly −3% for this period, which is economically implausible and therefore best treated as a model limitation: the Chow-Lin-interpolated activity proxy does not fully capture the structural break induced by the Donbas conflict, causing the demand-shock replacement to generate spurious negative values. For this episode, the CM estimate is the more credible reference. During the 2022 full-scale invasion, the gap narrows to −0.1pp (CM) and −2.3pp (SVAR), with the counterfactual slightly above actual inflation. This counterintuitive result is economically meaningful: the Euro Area itself was simultaneously absorbing a large energy-price shock, so ECB membership would have provided little additional insulation. Moreover, the Ukrainian government's active price controls, fuel subsidies, and informal anchoring to Western aid compressed realised inflation below what an open-price EA-equivalent trajectory would have implied. Consistent with Barro and Gordon (1983) and Giavazzi and Pagano (1988), the credibility-import benefit is front-loaded: the pre-2016 average gap (+3.54pp) exceeds the post-2016 gap (+1.91pp), as the NBU's adoption of inflation targeting in August 2015 partially converged Ukraine's monetary framework toward the ECB norm, structurally reducing the treatment intensity of hypothetical euro membership. Finally, following De Grauwe (2012), Euro Area membership would not have been an unambiguous gain: in 2014 and 2022, Ukraine avoided deeper crises precisely because it could devalue and impose capital controls, instruments unavailable under the euro. Without a national lender of last resort, the asymmetric shocks of 2014 and 2022 might instead have triggered sovereign debt distress, shifting the adjustment burden into a fiscal-financial crisis of potentially greater severity. During hard-peg plateaux (2000–2008, 2022–2023), the two series coincide by construction: Ukraine's monetary policy was already subordinated to an external anchor, leaving no independent monetary channel for EA membership to substitute, a consequence of the time-varying treatment formalised in Section 3b and consistent with Calvo and Reinhart (2002).
